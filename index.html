North Carolina and the Kansas question

Kansas cut income taxes and the bills kept coming. North Carolina is now cutting on a fixed schedule while aging infrastructure and federal cost shifts press in from several directions.

North Carolina's scheduled personal income tax rate, 2026–2034

Under S.B. 257, signed July 7, 2026. The rate falls from 3.99% to 2.99% on an automatic schedule.

What happened in Kansas

In 2012, Kansas Governor Sam Brownback signed sweeping income tax cuts, calling them a “real live experiment” to see whether lower taxes would stimulate the economy enough to produce more revenue. The plan erased the top income tax bracket and lowered rates across the board, and it exempted income from pass-through businesses like LLCs. Brownback argued the cuts would pay for themselves.

They didn’t.

According to the Kansas experiment entry on Wikipedia, growth generated only enough new revenue to offset 10 to 30 percent of most of the initial tax cut, which forced spending cuts to avoid deficits. Tax revenue consistently came in below expectations. The state Supreme Court ruled that lawmakers had to increase school funding, and one account says $1.9 billion was diverted from highway improvements between 2012 and 2017 to plug the hole left by falling income tax collections.

In 2017, a Republican-led legislature repealed most of the cuts with bipartisan supermajorities, overriding Brownback’s veto.

North Carolina’s version

North Carolina’s approach is slower and more cautious on paper, but it points the same direction. The flat individual rate is 3.99% in 2026. On July 7, 2026, Governor Stein signed S.B. 257, which progressively lowers the personal income tax rate from 3.99% to 2.99% over seven years: 3.49% for 2027 through 2029, 3.24% for 2030 through 2032, and 2.99% for 2033 and 2034.

The law repeals the earlier revenue-trigger cuts for those years and replaces them with an automatic schedule. After the rate reaches 2.99%, it authorizes up to two additional reductions between 2035 and 2040 if revenue thresholds are met.

The corporate side has gone even further. North Carolina has a flat 2 percent corporate income tax rate, one of the lowest in the nation and scheduled to phase down further. By law, the state will phase out the corporate income tax completely by 2030.

What the state isn’t investing in

The cuts are landing in a state that already invests little in its public schools. The National Education Association ranks North Carolina 46th in the nation for average teacher pay, and it was the only state where teacher pay was projected to drop this year. The latest budget includes an average 8% raise for teachers, but starting pay remains below the national average.

Roads, bridges and dams

The physical infrastructure tells a similar story. The American Society of Civil Engineers gave North Carolina’s infrastructure an overall C- in its January 2026 report card.

Dams

North Carolina has 7,448 dams, and the report estimates a funding gap of about $5.97 billion to maintain and repair those not rated satisfactory. Nearly half of that, $2.83 billion, is needed for high-hazard dams, the ones where a failure would threaten lives downstream. Hurricane Helene damaged more than 40 dams in the western part of the state, and many communities are still waiting for repairs or replacements.

Bridges

State bridge funding has grown from $150 million in FY2015 to $482 million in FY2025, but more than 4,800 bridges are over 50 years old, and over 6,300 are expected to be by 2035. Bridge funding needs are projected to reach $961 million by FY2027, leaving a $479 million shortfall that keeps widening.

The replacement of the Cape Fear Memorial Bridge is estimated to cost more than $1 billion. And the State is proposing tolls to pay for the lion’s share of the construction cost.

Roads

Nearly two-thirds of state roads are rated only “fair.” A report to lawmakers found that the statewide road score of 83 remains in the good range, but the most-trafficked roads are deteriorating. The same report said the maintenance budget would need to increase by more than 159% by 2030 to keep roads up. The gas tax, a primary funding source, is declining as more drivers switch to hybrid and electric vehicles.

Much of the recent progress came from Washington, not Raleigh. The federal Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act has provided $12.3 billion to projects statewide, helping close longstanding gaps. That federal money is a one-time boost, not a permanent funding stream.

The federal squeeze

The tax cuts aren’t happening in a vacuum. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act is shifting costs onto North Carolina just as state revenue is being dialed back.

SNAP

75%of administrative costs now paid by states, up from half

North Carolina is one of just ten states where counties administer the program, so the bill lands on county budgets.

The state’s county commissioners estimate $52 million in additional costs this fiscal year and about $69 million annually starting in 2027.

Beyond administration, states with SNAP error rates above 6% must begin paying 5% to 15% of benefit costs starting in October 2027. North Carolina had an error rate above 10% in 2024, which would put it in the 15% tier.

Small, poor counties have the least room to absorb it. Bladen County faces more than $397,000 in new administrative costs.

Even towns and cities that do not administer SNAP will lose revenue, as the overall sales tax distribution to the county will be reduced by the State of North Carolina.

Medicaid

~$50Bin federal funding lost over 10 years, by one analysis

State officials estimate more than 255,000 North Carolinians could lose coverage under the new work requirement, many because of paperwork rather than unemployment.

The law also limits the provider taxes the state uses to fund its share of expansion, which analysts warn could trip the state’s own trigger law and end Medicaid expansion for roughly 670,000 people.

NCDHHS says administration alone will cost the state an additional $44.4 million a year.

Why this is the Kansas question again

Kansas didn’t fail because of one bad tax cut. It failed because the revenue went away and the bills didn’t.

North Carolina now faces the same arithmetic from several directions at once: automatic state revenue reductions on a fixed schedule, deferred maintenance on aging bridges and dams, and federal changes that push more of the cost of food assistance and health care down to the state and its counties.

When the next lean year arrives, the squeeze will fall on the same places it fell in Kansas: schools, roads, and local services, in a state already near the bottom on education funding.
